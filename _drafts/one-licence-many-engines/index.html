One Licence, Many Engines

Drafted 25 October 2026 1,540 words
By Binary Outlook

Free software, AI, and the freedom to build.

There is something rather interesting about the way we rent cars.

A rental company is prepared to hand us the keys to a vehicle we may never have driven before. Its dimensions, controls, and handling might be unfamiliar. It might run on petrol or electricity. Yet we are generally expected to make the necessary adjustments and get on with the journey.

We do not begin by learning to drive all over again.

Our existing knowledge carries us a considerable distance. We need to understand what is different, recognise our limitations, and exercise reasonable judgment.

I find this an increasingly useful analogy for software development.

Moving between programming languages has often involved considerable friction. The underlying ideas might be familiar, but each language arrives with its own conventions, libraries, tooling, and preferred ways of organising a program.

Understanding what you want to build does not necessarily mean knowing how a particular ecosystem expects you to build it.

Modern development tools, documentation, package managers, and reusable libraries have already made these transitions easier. Generative AI has added another way into unfamiliar territory.

Today, I can open a repository written in a language I do not know particularly well and begin asking questions about its structure. I can investigate a compiler error or explore how a concept I understand in Go might be expressed in Rust.

When the explanation is inadequate, I can ask another question.

That last part is particularly useful. Documentation is generally rather less willing to explain itself a second time.

In my own work, this makes unfamiliar technologies easier to approach. It does not make the languages interchangeable, or make me equally competent in all of them. But it helps me begin.

There is, however, a much older achievement behind this experience that deserves more attention.

Before an assistant could help me understand somebody else’s software, that software had to be available for me to study and change.

The free software movement made those freedoms its central concern.

“Free” here refers to the freedoms of the person using the software: to run it for any purpose, study and modify its source, and redistribute original or modified copies. Software can be sold while still respecting these freedoms. The question is what its users are allowed to do with it. GNU’s free software definition sets out those freedoms.

This is also where the rental-car analogy reaches its limit.

Being handed the keys gives me permission to operate a vehicle. Free software offers something much broader: access to the workings, permission to alter them, and the ability to share the result under its licence.

GNU/Linux remains, to my mind, one of the most extraordinary expressions of that idea.

The Linux kernel, GNU tools and libraries, and contributions from many other projects form the foundations of complete operating systems. Distributions such as Debian undertake the considerable work of packaging and integrating those contributions into systems people can install, use, and maintain. Debian’s account of GNU/Linux describes how those pieces come together.

Consider what that makes possible.

A person can begin with a working operating system and investigate how it works. A group can adapt it to a particular purpose. Others can build on those changes, subject to the relevant licences. Each participant inherits the possibility of continuing work that began long before they arrived.

Nobody needs to build the whole thing alone.

Nor does everybody need to understand every component. The ability to inspect and modify a system can benefit people who never read its source themselves, because they can seek help from someone who does. Knowledge and responsibility can be shared.

That is an extraordinary arrangement.

Its success also requires much more than making source code available. Somebody must review changes, investigate failures, maintain documentation, coordinate releases, and decide how the pieces fit together.

In Coase’s Penguin, Yochai Benkler examined this kind of commons-based peer production, including the importance of integrating contributions and maintaining their quality. Collaboration at this scale is an organisational achievement as well as a technical one.

The freedoms involved also depend on deliberate choices about licensing.

Copyleft licences such as the GNU General Public License require those distributing covered software, including modified versions, to preserve the relevant freedoms for recipients. Other free software licences permit broader choices about the terms of downstream versions. These differences matter when we decide how our work will remain available to others. GNU’s explanation of copyleft describes the approach.

There are commitments beyond the licence, too. Debian’s Social Contract includes sharing improvements with upstream developers, keeping bug reports publicly accessible, and prioritising users and free software. These are practical commitments about how a shared system should be maintained.

This is the foundation on which I find AI assistance most interesting.

Having the right to study a large codebase does not make understanding it easy. There can be a considerable distance between downloading the source and knowing where to begin.

An assistant can help narrow that distance.

It can suggest where a behaviour is implemented, explain unfamiliar syntax, or help trace a path through several components. It can propose a small change and help devise a test for it. Its explanation may be mistaken, but the source provides something against which that explanation can be checked.

For me, this suggests a useful relationship between free software and AI: the former establishes freedoms that the latter may make easier to exercise.

A developer who would previously have avoided an unfamiliar repository might become willing to investigate a bug. Someone adapting a tool to a local need might be able to understand enough of it to produce a useful change.

If those changes are carefully checked and shared, the benefit can accumulate.

The person who once depended entirely on other people’s work may find a way to contribute to it.

Of course, producing a change is only part of the job.

An architecture built around class hierarchies, factories, and strategy patterns might feel natural in one Java application. Reproducing that structure mechanically in Rust could result in an awkward design that ignores the language’s strengths.

An assistant may generate something resembling the original implementation without establishing that it is a sensible one.

There is a difference between translating code and understanding why it was written that way.

The same applies at the level of a whole system. A web application might combine a TypeScript frontend, a Go backend, and a database. Assistance can make it easier to navigate those components. It does not settle questions about access control, data consistency, failure recovery, or maintenance.

A successful demonstration is not the same as a reliable system.

Frederick Brooks made a related distinction in No Silver Bullet: some difficulties arise from expressing and implementing software, while others belong to the conceptual problem itself. Better tools can help with the former without making the latter disappear. That remains a useful distinction when evaluating what an assistant has actually accomplished.

There is another question that the free software perspective encourages us to ask: how much control do we have over the assistant itself?

A proprietary service can help us modify free software. The usefulness of that service does not give us the same freedoms over the service or its underlying system.

If our ability to understand and maintain a project becomes dependent on access to a particular provider, that dependency deserves attention. I would like these tools to increase our knowledge and our ability to continue the work, including when the assistant is unavailable.

This also matters for people learning to program.

Many small tasks are valuable precisely because they force us to investigate something we do not yet understand. Fixing a bug, writing a test, and explaining a change can develop judgment that is difficult to acquire by accepting a finished answer.

We should preserve those opportunities as assistance improves.

Free software offers real systems to study and meaningful problems to work on. An assistant might help a newcomer approach that work, but learning still requires engagement: checking an explanation against the source, understanding why a test fails, and responding thoughtfully to review.

Experienced contributors also need time and support to provide that review. Generating more patches is useful only when the work of evaluating and maintaining them remains manageable.

What excites me, then, is the possibility that more people will be able to participate in software they previously found inaccessible.

GNU/Linux already demonstrates how much can be achieved when people can build on one another’s work. Its example should shape what we hope to gain from the next generation of tools.

We can use assistance to explore unfamiliar systems, deepen our understanding, and make contributions that others can continue. We can also support the maintainers whose work makes that participation possible.

The driving analogy still has something to offer. Experience can carry us into unfamiliar vehicles, provided we learn their important characteristics and recognise when we need help.

Free software takes the possibility further. We can examine the machinery, improve it, and pass on what we have learned.

The machinery has become easier to work with. We should use that opportunity to understand more of it, improve it, and leave others the freedom to do the same.
