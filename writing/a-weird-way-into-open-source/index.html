A Weird Way into Open Source

22 September 2026 735 words
By Binary Outlook

I got into open source partly because I needed to graduate.

I was doing the International Baccalaureate in high school, which meant completing Creativity, Activity, Service, usually shortened to CAS. It sat alongside the subjects we were studying, with its own requirements, reflections, and the occasional need to explain to a coordinator what exactly we had been doing with our time.

The service component produced quite a variety of answers. Some classmates put in dozens of hours volunteering, collecting plastic bottles and turning up for activities that began before sunrise or continued after sunset. There was considerable effort involved.

I read the requirements rather carefully.

What caught my attention was the emphasis on responding to a real need. Somebody had to need the thing you were doing. As I understood it, the hours were a poor substitute for that basic condition. An activity did not become useful simply because I had spent a long time on it.

At that point, I could barely write a for loop. I did, however, spend enough time online to know that there were people building things together, and that their lists of unfinished work could be remarkably long.

Some of that work involved translation.

A programmer could make something useful to people around the world without speaking the languages those people used. Documentation needed translating. Interfaces had missing strings. A project could be technically impressive and still be difficult to approach if you could not comfortably read its instructions.

Here was an existing need, expressed by the people responsible for the project. I happened to be able to help with some of it.

The work could also be done in an air-conditioned room. I considered this a favourable arrangement.

This was before ChatGPT. Machine translation existed, of course, and I would not pretend that every sentence required an act of literary genius. But its awkwardness was familiar enough. Someone still needed to work out what a phrase meant in context, whether a technical term had survived the journey, and whether the result sounded like something a person would actually say.

So I translated a few things.

Then I translated a few more, across different repositories. I spent enough time looking for opportunities that GitHub’s repository discovery pages became part of the process. I would browse around, see what people were making, and look for something I could contribute.

Added together, the translation work may have amounted to less than a working day. My coordinator accepted those contributions as service without much difficulty.

That was my experience at my school. I have no idea how the same proposal would fare with somebody else’s coordinator today.

I also cannot offer a particularly heroic account of my intentions. I wanted to satisfy a requirement. I liked computers. I had found something useful that I could do without standing outside in the heat. These considerations coexisted quite comfortably.

The translations were still needed.

I find that part more interesting now than I did then. We often tell stories about participation backwards, starting with a conviction and arranging the earlier events as evidence that it was always there. My route was considerably less orderly. A school requirement gave me a reason to look; looking revealed work I could do.

And the work was sitting surprisingly close to the surface. I did not have to understand the entire application before improving a small part of the experience of using it. Knowing another language was enough to make certain problems visible to me, even while most of the code remained beyond my understanding.

There must be many people who encounter a project from a similarly sideways direction. They need it for a class. They are irritated by one particular bug. They want a feature for a hobby the maintainer has never heard of. Perhaps they are looking for something to put on a résumé.

A project has room for quite a few reasons to be there.

Maintaining it still takes judgment, and somebody has to review what arrives. But I like the fact that participation can begin with such an ordinary coincidence: there is something unfinished, and a person nearby knows how to finish that particular thing.

In my case, “nearby” meant somewhere on the Internet, sitting comfortably indoors.

I needed something for my school record. Somebody else needed a translation. For a little while, that was enough to get us working on the same thing.
