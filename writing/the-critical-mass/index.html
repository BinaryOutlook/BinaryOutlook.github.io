The Critical Mass

22 September 2026 919 words
By Binary Outlook

I was rather enthusiastic about physics and mathematics in high school. This was before ChatGPT, when an unanswered question could remain inconveniently unanswered for quite some time.

One idea worth borrowing from that world is the limit on what a structure can support.

A white dwarf is held up against gravity by electron degeneracy pressure, a consequence of quantum mechanics. That support has a limit. In the standard picture, the Chandrasekhar limit is roughly 1.4 times the mass of the Sun. A white dwarf cannot keep gaining mass indefinitely and remain stable in the same way.

Software has no corresponding universal constant. We can rearrange it, divide responsibilities, and change the machinery around it. Still, I find the underlying question useful: how much can we add before the things holding a project together become inadequate?

At the beginning, adding things feels almost unambiguously good.

Another feature makes the application more useful. Another contributor brings knowledge and time. More users mean more people finding problems, requesting improvements, and occasionally solving something themselves.

Eventually, there may be enough activity for the project to acquire a life beyond its original author. Questions receive answers while the maintainer is elsewhere. Somebody understands a subsystem better than the person who started it. People begin building other things that depend on it.

That is one attractive meaning of critical mass: enough participation for something to keep moving.

The same growth also creates obligations. Features interact. Contributors make different assumptions. A change that looks harmless in one corner produces a surprising result somewhere else.

The project has gained more than the sum of its additions.

In The Cathedral and the Bazaar, Eric S. Raymond discusses Brooks’s warning about the cost of adding people to a software project. Available labour grows with the number of developers, while the number of possible communication relationships grows much faster—quadratically in the simple all-to-all model.

Possible relationships are not the same as necessary conversations. Much of the work of organising a project is deciding which conversations actually need to happen.

That is part of what interests me about an ecosystem such as GNU/Linux. Decades of development have produced something far beyond what one person could reasonably hold in their head. Yet people continue to work on particular parts, relying on boundaries, conventions, and the work of others.

Nobody has to understand everything. Enough people have to understand the right things, and the connections between those things need care.

Now we have agents that can read code, propose changes, and carry out substantial stretches of implementation.

It is tempting to look at that as a straightforward increase in capacity. More work can happen at once. A task that previously seemed too tedious to begin suddenly becomes approachable.

I share that enthusiasm. I also wonder what happens when a project gains code faster than it gains shared understanding.

An agent can produce a plausible change while missing the reason a nearby piece of code is deliberately awkward. It can solve the immediate problem by adding another abstraction to a project that already has three ways of doing the same thing. Several agents can each complete their assignments and leave behind a rather difficult integration problem.

The familiar coordination problem has acquired some new participants.

This makes context management interesting to me as a maintenance problem. A repository contains code, but it also contains decisions: which behaviour must survive, where responsibilities belong, what has already been tried, and what evidence is needed before calling a change correct.

Some of those decisions are written down. Others are scattered through commits, issues, comments, and somebody’s memory.

An agent arriving at a task has to reconstruct enough of that situation to act sensibly.

The way we organise the project affects how expensive that reconstruction becomes. Clear module boundaries can narrow the search. A useful explanation near an unusual decision can prevent repeated investigation. A precise task can establish what success means before implementation begins.

An AGENTS.md file can help with some of this. It can point to the relevant checks, explain conventions, and record constraints that would otherwise have to be rediscovered.

It can also grow into an impressive collection of instructions that nobody has kept current.

That is the awkward part. Every explanation we add creates something else that can become wrong. More context can help, but it also gives the reader more material to reconcile. A rule that once prevented a mistake may survive long after the circumstances that justified it.

I would like the project’s internal directions to make necessary information easier to find and easier to trust. That seems a more useful ambition than making every agent read everything.

It should help the humans returning to the repository, too. Forgetting why something works is hardly an exclusively artificial habit.

Perhaps that is how we move our own version of the critical mass upwards. We improve the structures that let the project remain understandable as it grows. We make responsibilities easier to divide, assumptions easier to inspect, and mistakes easier to detect.

I would want to judge those improvements by what happens afterwards: less repeated discovery, fewer incompatible changes, and a reasonable amount of time spent checking the result. A shorter prompt or a lower token count alone would tell only part of the story.

The appeal of more powerful tools is that they let us attempt more. I have no desire to lose that ambition.

I would like to keep building things that can survive the next good idea.
