The Critical Mass

22 September 2026 1,109 words
By Binary Outlook Updated 23 September 2026

I was rather enthusiastic about physics and mathematics in high school. I still find it difficult to resist borrowing a physical idea when trying to understand something else, even when the analogy eventually needs to be handled with considerable care.

The Chandrasekhar limit is one such temptation.

A white dwarf is held up against gravity by electron degeneracy pressure, a consequence of quantum mechanics. Adding mass makes it more compact. Under the standard assumptions, there is a limit—roughly 1.4 times the mass of the Sun—beyond which that mechanism cannot support a stable white dwarf. NASA’s explanation is a useful place to begin.

What interests me is the relationship between accumulation and support. A structure can accommodate a great deal, while still depending on conditions that will not hold indefinitely.

Software has no corresponding universal constant. We can reorganise a project, divide responsibilities, and change the tools used to maintain it. But I find myself asking a similar question: how much can we add before the things holding the project together become inadequate?

The phrase critical mass pulls me in two directions here.

A project may need enough users and contributors to acquire a life beyond its original author. Someone answers a question while the maintainer is elsewhere. Somebody understands one subsystem unusually well. Other projects begin depending on the work.

That kind of accumulation can make continued development easier.

It also creates relationships that need attention. Features interact. Contributors make different assumptions. A decision that seemed local becomes relevant to a part of the system its author had never considered.

In The Cathedral and the Bazaar, Eric S. Raymond discusses Brooks’s warning about adding people to a late software project. The additional labour comes with communication costs. In the simple all-to-all model, the number of possible relationships grows quadratically with the number of participants.

Possible relationships are not the same as necessary conversations. A project’s organisation affects how much coordination any particular change requires.

An ecosystem such as GNU/Linux makes me reluctant to locate the limit in what one person can understand. Its accumulated work has long exceeded that. People maintain particular parts and depend on other people’s understanding elsewhere. Some of what holds it together must therefore exist in the boundaries between those parts, and in the expectations people can reasonably have of one another.

Now agents can read repositories and produce substantial changes. I am interested in how much further that lets us go. I am also interested in what happens when code grows faster than the understanding needed to maintain it.

One answer is to write more things down.

There are reasons a piece of code looks the way it does. Certain behaviours need to survive. Some approaches have already been tried. If every new contributor or agent has to reconstruct all of that, useful time disappears into repeated investigation.

An explanation can save the next reader some of the work.

But the explanation is another description of the project, and the project keeps changing. Someone now has to determine whether the description still fits.

The unlisted articles on this site provide a small example. Originally, the word-count checker expected every article to have a corresponding entry in the Writing index. That was a sensible expectation while every article was meant to be listed.

Once some articles could be unlisted, the expectation needed to become conditional. Listed articles still required an index entry; unlisted ones required its absence. The authoring instructions changed alongside the checker. The change to the checker and instructions was small.

Looking back at it, I find the less obvious cases more interesting. Here, an automated check made the old assumption explicit. What happens when an equally reasonable assumption survives only in a paragraph of instructions?

“Every article should appear in the index” would still sound sensible to someone arriving at the repository. Following it could undo the very distinction we had just introduced.

This is where I start to separate missing context from outdated context.

With missing context, a reader may have an unanswered question. An old explanation can supply an answer whose confidence has outlasted its applicability. Giving an agent more material to read does not, by itself, distinguish the two.

That does not make missing context preferable. A reader can invent a bad explanation to fill a gap, and an older note may be the only surviving account of why something was done. Removing it could make the project harder to understand.

In the article example, the earlier rule still tells us something useful. It explains why the checker would have objected, and what kind of publication workflow the site originally assumed. The rule’s history remains relevant even after its scope changes.

So perhaps age is too crude a way to judge an explanation. I would rather know what circumstances make it applicable, and whether it describes a present requirement, a past decision, or an idea that was never carried through.

An AGENTS.md file can contain all three while making them look remarkably similar.

I can imagine responding by adding more qualifications: this rule applies to these files, except under these conditions, for this reason. That may help. It also gives us another set of conditions to keep current.

The supporting structure has acquired some weight of its own.

I do not yet know how far that observation takes the analogy. Software gives us opportunities to change its organisation that a white dwarf does not have. Still, I suspect there is a limit to how much complexity we can compensate for by attaching further explanations to it.

Some knowledge may belong in a check that fails when an assumption stops holding. Some may belong near the code it explains. Some may be valuable precisely because it records a disagreement that no automated check could settle.

Choosing between those places requires an understanding of the project too. We have not escaped the problem merely by distributing it.

What I would like to try is fairly small: take a few instructions that seem useful, make their conditions clearer, and see whether a fresh reader or agent makes better decisions with them. I would want to notice what still has to be rediscovered, and whether the qualifications themselves become another source of confusion.

That would tell me more than the length of the instruction file, or how confidently an agent says it has understood it.

I am still attracted to the possibility of doing more with better tools. I would like to understand what we are adding to the maintenance burden along the way.

I would like to keep building things that can survive the next good idea.
