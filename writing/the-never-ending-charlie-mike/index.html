The Never-ending Charlie Mike

22 September 2026 783 words
By Binary Outlook

Charlie Mike is not an expression I instinctively associate with good news.

For anyone unfamiliar with it, it means “continue mission.” Two words, conveniently shortened, which can arrive just as you have begun entertaining the possibility that you might be finished.

There is usually more to do.

I have developed a somewhat warmer relationship with the idea when it comes to software. A project begins somewhere, with particular people trying to solve a particular problem. Ten years later, it may still be moving, although almost everything around it has changed.

The original developers could hardly have planned all of that. Their priorities change. Users find unexpected purposes for the software. Features that once seemed essential become awkward historical baggage, while some obscure capability turns out to be the reason a small group refuses to use anything else.

Sometimes the people who started the project simply have other things to do.

That seems reasonable enough. An application can occupy a substantial place in somebody’s life without its author having intended to maintain it for the rest of theirs.

The difficulty comes when the author’s departure also decides the future for everybody else.

A product reaches its end of life. A company changes direction. The returns no longer justify the work. Those decisions may make perfect sense to whoever makes them, while leaving behind people whose needs have barely changed at all.

I still need the thing. You no longer want to make the thing. There ought to be somewhere we can go from here.

One example that caught my attention was Airline Club, an open-source airline simulation with roots in the mid-2010s. I was particularly impressed by how it approached passengers. They have preferences concerning price, service quality, loyalty, and other factors, and the simulation considers alternative routes through the network.

That is the sort of detail that makes an aviation game interesting to me. Aircraft are appealing, naturally, but there is something satisfying about a model that gives people reasons to board them. The network begins to matter as a network.

Then there is MyFly Club, a fork that Spencer has helped develop. It builds on the same foundation while taking its own direction. Its development updates cover changes to airline economics, progression, balancing, and the interface. Somebody found enough value in the existing work to keep building upon it.

And the original Airline Club has continued receiving changes too.

I like that detail. There does not have to be an abandoned repository, a dramatic departure, or a rescue operation. People can simply want different things.

The mission has acquired another interpretation.

A fork gives that disagreement somewhere practical to go. Perhaps one group wants a more demanding simulation. Another wants a friendlier introduction. Someone else has an idea too experimental to belong in the main project. They can begin with years of accumulated work and see where their own priorities lead.

There is still work involved, of course. Source code does not pay the hosting bill, investigate a broken deployment, or explain an undocumented decision from six years ago. Somebody has to take responsibility for the version they want to exist.

But the opportunity is there. The original maintainer’s willingness to continue is one part of the picture, alongside the interest and ability of everyone who comes afterwards.

That changes how I think about a project’s lifespan.

Its name may change. Its most active contributors may never meet the people who wrote the first version. A feature added for one purpose may survive because it turns out to serve another. Some branches will disappear. Others may become more useful than anyone expected.

There is no particular reason all of this should remain under one command.

I find some of the same appeal in Richard Stallman’s argument for free software in schools. He places considerable weight on students being able to inspect software, learn from it, and share what they have learned. I agree with that emphasis. A program can become something a student is allowed to question and change, and perhaps eventually take responsibility for.

A person who starts by asking how something works may later decide it ought to work differently.

Somewhere in the background, a server receives another push. Another branch appears. Somebody opens a pull request against a project whose first author has long since moved on to something else.

If servers had feelings, they might have stronger opinions about our endless Charlie Mikes.

For the people using the software, though, I find it reassuring. The next person can pick it up with their own reasons, their own interests, and their own idea of what should happen next.

The mission continues. Who carries it forward remains an open question.
