The Never-ending Charlie Mike

22 September 2026 947 words
By Binary Outlook Updated 23 September 2026

Charlie Mike is not an expression I instinctively associate with good news.

For anyone unfamiliar with it, it means “continue mission.” Two words, conveniently shortened, which can arrive just as you have begun entertaining the possibility that you might be finished.

There is usually more to do.

The expression becomes rather more appealing to me when applied to software. A project can remain useful long after its original developers have changed priorities, found other interests, or become thoroughly tired of maintaining it. Somebody else may still want to carry on.

But I am beginning to wonder how much the word continue conceals.

One project that caught my attention was Airline Club, an open-source airline simulation with roots in the mid-2010s. What interested me was its treatment of passengers. They have preferences concerning price, service quality, loyalty, and other factors, and the simulation considers alternative routes through the network.

Aircraft are appealing, naturally. But a fleet is only part of what makes an airline interesting. There are people trying to get somewhere, and several possible ways of getting them there. A route has consequences for the routes around it.

That is the sort of behaviour I want to think about in an aviation game. If a connection becomes less attractive, where do those passengers go? If I offer something cheaper, which travellers should care? I like the possibility of tracing an outcome back through the assumptions that produced it.

A simulation can give me plenty of numbers without giving me much to wonder about. A model of people choosing between alternatives offers something more.

Then there is MyFly Club, a fork that Spencer has helped develop. It takes an existing foundation and continues working on it, with changes to the interface, economics, progression, and other parts of the game.

The original Airline Club has continued receiving changes as well. There are several versions of the future available at once.

That makes the example more interesting to me than a straightforward story about rescuing an abandoned project.

One MyFly Club update, dated 20 March 2026, describes a correction to baseline demand that affects travel rates. The developers expect demand and reputation to be lower as a result, while describing the change as improving the simulation’s accuracy and balance.

It is a small entry, but it leaves me with something to think about. A player might see fewer passengers and experience the change as a loss. Someone interested in the model might welcome it—provided the earlier traffic really was being generated for the wrong reasons.

The release note tells me what the developers intended. It does not establish how the revised demand was calibrated, or how well it behaves across different networks. Those would take a closer look.

I am interested in that gap. What counts as an improvement depends partly on what we want the simulation to explain. A correction may bring it closer to its existing aims. A different change might make the game more enjoyable by deliberately simplifying something.

Both could arrive under the heading of continued development.

I keep calling MyFly Club a continuation of Airline Club, which is a reasonable description of its code’s ancestry. Whether that also describes its purpose is a question I would want to examine through the decisions being made.

When a fork changes what a project is trying to do, what exactly is being continued?

Some of the answer may be straightforward: a passenger model, a collection of aircraft data, years of accumulated fixes. Other parts are harder to identify. A familiar way of playing. An expectation about how much realism should matter. A particular kind of problem that the developers find worth their attention.

The people maintaining a fork may preserve those things, or find them less interesting than I do. Having the source gives them room to make that choice.

I find this both reassuring and slightly uncomfortable. When I hope that software will survive, I probably have something more particular in mind than another repository with related code. I want some part of the experience that attracted me to remain available. The next maintainer may be carrying forward a different part.

An original developer cannot reasonably be expected to resolve that tension forever. Interests change; time becomes scarce. Users can continue needing a tool after its author has stopped wanting to maintain it. Open source gives that situation somewhere to go, although somebody still has to do the work.

This is also where I find something appealing in Richard Stallman’s argument for free software in schools. I am interested in what happens when understanding a program leads a student to question one of its assumptions.

With an airline simulation, for example, reading how passengers choose their journeys might lead someone to wonder about a different model. The program becomes a place to investigate that question. The possibility of changing it matters even before the alternative turns out to be any good.

Somewhere along the way, another branch appears. Another pull request follows. If servers had feelings, they might have considerably less affection for our endless Charlie Mikes.

For the people involved, there is still a choice about what deserves to continue.

I know why I am glad that choice exists. I am less certain how much of a project must remain familiar before I would call its development a continuation of what I liked. With Airline Club, I would probably begin by looking at the passengers again: what they are choosing, what affects those choices, and whether the decisions I make as a player still have reasons I can investigate.

That gives me something more specific to look for than whether the latest commit is recent.
